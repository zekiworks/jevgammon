# Backgammon: Rules and Strategy

## Rules

### Setup

The board has 24 points in a horseshoe-shaped track, 12 on each side. Each player numbers the points from their own side, 1 to 24, and moves from the 24-point toward the 1-point; the two players move in opposite directions. Each player starts with 15 checkers: two on the 24-point, five on the 13-point (the midpoint), three on the 8-point, and five on the 6-point.

Points 1-6 are your home board and points 7-12 your outer board. The 7-point is the bar point.

### Moving

- **Starting:** each player rolls one die. The higher number moves first, playing both dice. Equal numbers are rolled again. After that, players alternate, rolling two dice each turn.
- **Using the dice:** move one checker by each die. One checker may take both moves, as long as each step lands on a legal point. A roll of 6-3 moves one checker six and another (or the same) checker three.
- **Doubles** are played four times: 5-5 is four moves of five.
- **Using the whole roll:** you must use both dice if you can. If only one die can be played, you must play the higher one when possible. If neither can be played, the turn is lost.

### Hitting and entering

- A checker may land on an empty point, a point holding your own checkers, or a point holding exactly one opposing checker (a blot). Landing on a blot hits it and sends it to the bar.
- A checker may never land on a point held by two or more opposing checkers, so no point ever holds checkers of both players.
- A player with checkers on the bar must enter them all before moving anything else. Checkers enter in the opponent's home board: a 1 enters on your 24-point (the opponent's 1-point), a 2 on your 23-point, and so on up to a 6 on your 19-point. Entering on a blot hits it.
- If no die matches an open point, you dance and the turn is lost. Against a closed board you cannot enter at all until the opponent opens a point.

### Bearing off

Once all 15 of your checkers are in your home board, you may bear them off: a 1 removes a checker from the 1-point, a 2 from the 2-point, and so on. A die higher than your highest occupied point removes a checker from that highest point. For example, with 6-5, no checkers on the 6-point, and two on the 5-point, both checkers come off the 5-point. A checker hit during the bear-off must enter and come home before bearing off continues.

### Scoring

The first player to bear off all 15 checkers wins:

| Result | When | Points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single game | The loser has borne off at least one checker | 1 |
| Gammon | The loser has borne off no checkers | 2 |
| Backgammon | The loser has borne off no checkers and still has one on the bar or in the winner's home board | 3 |

## Strategy principles

Backgammon is a race to bear off first, but the straightest path is rarely the best one. Beginners tend to avoid leaving blots and hit whenever they can; neither habit makes a good plan.

- **Blots and points:** a blot is exposed and goes back to the start if hit. Two or more checkers make a safe point that can also block the opponent.
- **Distribution:** spread checkers evenly over the points you hold. Three checkers on each of two points beat four and two. Rarely put six checkers on one point and almost never more. Even distribution gives you more good rolls.
- **Exposure:** leaving shots early to build a strong position is fine. Be more careful as the opponent's home board grows, since entering gets harder. Anchors in the opponent's home board let you play more boldly. Even against a weak board, leave no more than four blots. When well ahead in the race or position, cut your exposure to keep the lead.
- **Blocking:** make points without gaps directly in front of the opponent's back checkers. The most valuable early points, in rough order, are your 5-, 4- and 7-points. Six in a row is a full prime, and checkers behind it cannot escape while it holds.
- **Hitting:** hit the opponent's most advanced checkers, or blots they want to cover to make an important point. Hit only when it helps: with two opposing checkers already on the bar, making another home-board point is usually worth more than hitting a third. Do not hit if it leaves you more exposed than your opponent. When genuinely in doubt, hit.
- **Anchors:** an anchor gives you a safe point to enter on and keeps the opponent from closing their board. Early on, aim for the high points in the opponent's home board: your 20- and 21-points (their 5- and 4-points). Far behind in the race, the low points (your 22-, 23- and 24-points) gain value, because your plan becomes building your board and waiting for a shot. With two anchors, keep them on adjacent points.

## Game plans

The goal is always to bring your checkers around and off before your opponent. Which plan fits depends on the early rolls and on your opponent's plan: two early 6-5s point toward a race, while checkers hit early point toward a holding game or a back game.

### Running game

Bring every checker home and bear off as fast as possible. Choose it when your pip count is lower than your opponent's and your checkers are past theirs, or likely to get past. Racing while behind in the pip count has little chance.

### Holding game

Keep an anchor high in the opponent's board, usually their 5-point (your 20-point) or their bar point. It is often the best plan when behind in the race, because those anchors give the most chances to hit as the opponent brings checkers home; anchors further back are much weaker. Watch the opponent's distribution: holding only the 8- and 13-points, they will often have to leave a shot on the way home, but extra landing points in their outer board cut your chances sharply.

### Priming game

Build a prime, ideally six points in a row, to trap the opponent's checkers behind it, and hit blots to put more checkers behind the wall. The prime can start anywhere from your 2-point to your 11-point and be rolled forward into your home board as the bear-off approaches.

**Battle of primes:** when the opponent has a prime, get your trapped checkers to its front edge, where a single number escapes; against a 5-point prime you need a 6, and against a full prime there is no escape. When both sides hold strong primes, what matters is the length of each prime, how many checkers are trapped, and whether they sit at the front. All else equal, the player behind in the pip count is favored: if neither side escapes, the player ahead in the race must break their prime first.

### Blitz

Attack: hit repeatedly to keep the opponent on the bar while making points in your home board, aiming to close them out. A blitz usually starts from an early roll where you hit and the opponent fails to enter, or you hit two checkers and they enter only one. It is a poor plan when the opponent's home board is about as strong as yours, because you are taking risks and cannot afford to be hit and dance. Blitzes are committal: once you start, you must keep hitting and taking chances to make points. If the attack fails, because the opponent anchors or too many of your checkers are hit, you can easily end up losing. A successful blitz often wins a gammon.

### Two-way forward game

A hybrid of the blitz and the priming game: with a three- or four-point prime already built, you attack. If the opponent anchors, you aim to have extended your prime along the way and win the priming game. It is very strong, because when the dice go badly for one plan they often go well for the other.

### Back game

Hold two or more points in the opponent's home board, keep at least 10 checkers forward to contain a checker you hit, and wait for the opponent to roll awkwardly and leave a shot late in the game. It needs good timing: you must be far enough behind in the race to keep your position while you wait. With marginal timing (50 to 70 pips behind), back games on higher points, such as the opponent's 2- and 4-points or 3- and 4-points, are much stronger than those using the ace point. The best combinations are usually the opponent's 1- and 3-, 2- and 3-, or 2- and 4-points; the 1- and 2-points are weak.

A back game is a last resort, not an opening plan. It often arises on its own after a failed blitz leaves many of your checkers hit, and even then hitting an opposing checker early and trapping it is usually better. Commit fully only once you hold two back points and are about 70 pips or more behind. Failed back games often lose a gammon or backgammon.

## Match rules

- **Rounds:** the match is {rounds} rounds. Each round is two games with identical dice: in the second game the players swap seats and the same sequence of rolls is repeated, so both players face the same luck. The player who moved first in the first game moves second in the second.
- **Score:** the points of every game (1, 2 or 3) add up to the match score. The first player to reach 5 points wins the match, but the mirror game of that round is always played first: if the round ends with one player ahead and at least one player at 5 or more, the player ahead wins the match. If the score is tied when the match would end (after the planned rounds, or level at 5 or more after a round), one extra round is played; a tie after that is a draw. In a playoff, extra rounds are played until one player leads after a round, so it cannot end in a draw.
- **Turn limit:** a game that has not ended after {max_turns} turns (both players' turns counted) ends there. The player with the lower pip count wins a single game (1 point); with equal pip counts, the player due to roll next wins.
- **Thinking time:** each decision first gets a free delay of {delay}, which does not accumulate; only time beyond it comes out of your bank of {bank} per game. Both banks are full again at the start of every game. A roll with a single legal play is played for you instantly. If your bank runs out while you decide, you lose that game for 1 point, whatever the position.

## Your turn

Each time you have a choice to make, you get the match score, the turn and the clocks, the position from your side, the race, the last 10 moves of this game, the dice, and the complete numbered list of legal plays for your roll, each with what the position looks like after it:

```text
  turn {turn}, {you | opponent} rolled {die1}-{die2}: {play | no legal move}
  ...

Which of the following moves makes a point in front of the opponent's checkers, or reduces your single pieces, or gets you closer to your home?

Legal plays:
1. {play}: hits {n}; makes the {p} point; leaves {n} blots, the opponent hits with {n} of 36 rolls; you hold {n} points ({n} in your home board), longest prime {n}; pips: you {n}, opponent {n}
2. ...
```

- Points are always in your numbering. A point listed under the opponent's checkers is that point counted from your side. The opponent's moves in the move list are also in your numbering: it moves from your 1-point toward your 24-point.
- A play lists one from/to step per checker: `13/7` moves a checker from your 13-point to your 7-point, `bar/22` enters from the bar on your 22-point, `6/off` bears a checker off, and `*` marks a hit. Plays that reach the same position are listed once.
- Every listed play is legal; you only choose among them. Rolls with no legal play or exactly one are played without asking you.
- What follows each play is computed exactly, for the position after it:
  - **hits:** opponent checkers it sends to the bar.
  - **makes:** points that now hold two or more of your checkers and did not before ("makes no new point" if none).
  - **blots and hitting rolls:** your single checkers left, and how many of the opponent's 36 possible rolls next turn have a legal play that hits at least one of them.
  - **points held, home board, longest prime:** your points with two or more checkers, how many of them are in your home board (points 1-6), and the longest run of such points in a row.
  - **pips:** both pip counts after the play; a hit adds to the opponent's.

## Expected output

Reply with JSON only, nothing before or after it:

```json
{"choice": N}
```

`N` is the number of the chosen play, from 1 to the number of legal plays. A reply that is not valid JSON, or whose number is out of range, is returned to you with the reason, up to three replies in all; after that the first legal play is made for you. Choose the strongest play: the one that gives you the best chance of winning the match, taking the score, the race and the position into account.
